\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Results follow the measurement chain: reference and twin audits, the
observable horizon and error floor, the pause experiment, and finally the
estimators.

\subsection{Measurement-Chain Audits}
Static position repeatability is 0.04--0.06~mm per axis and rotation
repeatability is 0.008--0.035$^\circ$. At 0.25-px corner noise, Monte Carlo
median position spread is 0.80~mm on validation and 0.72~mm on test; rotation
spread is 0.23$^\circ$ and 0.19$^\circ$, respectively
(Fig.~\ref{fig:uncertainty}). Combining static and corner terms by
(\ref{eq:uncertainty}) gives a representative position
per-axis $U_{\rm op}$ ($k_{\rm c}=2$) of approximately 1.4--1.6~mm for a frame, excluding the
unavailable board/intrinsic tolerances.

\centering

\caption{Reference characterization. Tilt errors produce millimeter-scale
translation changes while remaining weakly visible in reprojection.}
\label{fig:uncertainty}
\end{figure}

Dynamic holdout depends strongly on frame rate: median position residual is
0.27--0.31~mm at 29~fps, 1.06--1.89~mm at 25~fps, and 3.06--5.19~mm at
10~fps. At the latter rate, median rotation residual is 0.96--1.31$^\circ$.
The tilt sensitivity is 3.4--3.7~mm/deg but only 0.13--0.18~px/deg in
reprojection, so the 1.5-px reprojection gate does not bound pose error.

The rigidity recording yields held-out axis correlations 0.78/0.92/1.00,
magnitude correlation 0.95, and scale 0.99; two earlier recordings made
before the mount was secured fail the test and are excluded. Constraining
PnP rotation to the gyroscope still shifts the test reference by 8.1~mm
median (21.4~mm p90). Training on those contaminated labels raises apparent
0.2-s test $R^2$ from 0.04 to 0.24, consistent with label leakage.

\paragraph{Twin audit.}
All 95 selected inertial sequences and 25 rendered sequences pass the
automated audit. Ideal/noisy recomputation agrees below
$5\times10^{-5}$~g and $10^{-4}$~deg/s per axis. Rendered corner error is
0.252~px median and 0.842~px p90. Fig.~\ref{fig:domain} shows why an initial
twin failed: its median angular rate was three times the real value
(14.1 against 4.3$^\circ$/s) and its translational acceleration was too small.
Retuning these distributions changed single-model real-validation $R^2$ from
0.326 to 0.403.

\subsection{Observable Horizon and the Empirical Error Floor}
\label{sec:limit}
The displacement horizon is the dominant observability parameter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limit}(a)). For the earlier body-frame Conv--BiGRU (one seed),
the held-out test does not beat zero motion at 0.2~s, and validation $R^2$ rises through 0.177, 0.203, 0.265, and 0.343 for 0.5, 1, 2,
and 3~s, then saturates at 0.339 for 4~s. The selected setting is therefore
$h=3$~s with 2~s context on each side.

At that horizon conventional model changes no longer give a robust gain.
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} lists the fixed-validation choices. Excluding the
deliberately time-reversed data, the Group-A means span 31.89--33.24~mm;
the five-seed reference itself spans 29.80--31.40~mm after the curated
extra-train data are added. Counting the earlier Conv--BiGRU sweeps, more than
forty variants---capacity, regularization, loss, supervision density,
augmentation, pooling, context length, coarse branches, pretraining, input
axes and extra data---show no consistent improvement beyond this seed and
data-selection variation. Time reversal reaches 34.20~mm against 32.56~mm at
the same weight decay, suggesting that the real sensor chain is not
time-symmetric.

The plateau has a clear signature (Fig.~\ref{fig:limit}(b)). For the fixed-split
PhysNet ensemble (31.46~mm on validation), the residual correlates with the
reference displacement at $\varrho=-0.88/-0.78/-0.92$ per axis, the regression
slope of prediction on reference is only 0.37/0.48/0.20, and the median
predicted magnitude is 0.44 of the reference. The estimator returns a shrunk,
conditional-mean displacement, which is what (\ref{eq:decomp}) predicts when a
component of the target is not determined by the inputs.

Attributing a quantified share of the residual to ${\bf v}(t_a)$ requires an
independent velocity measurement, which the 10-fps reference cannot supply
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limit}(c)). An affine correction by the mean reference velocity
over the 7-s window removes 14.1\% of the error when fitted and scored on the
same pairs, but only 4.3\% when fitted on the other validation recording, and
this proxy contains the target displacement itself, so part of its effect is
the shrinkage above. A proxy built from the two context segments alone removes
1.8\% in-sample and increases the error by 6.5\% across recordings, and
${\bf v}(t_a)$ differentiated from the reference over $\pm0.3$~s is dominated by
PnP noise (mean $\|{\bf v}(t_a)\Delta t_{ab}\|=75.8$~mm against a mean displacement
of 35.6~mm). The decomposition therefore identifies the initial velocity as a
component the window cannot determine, but the present data do not measure how
much of the 30-mm residual it causes; residual bias and gravity leakage after
rotation compensation, reference noise at 10~fps and the anisotropic training
motion are the other candidates. Longer context did not help either: the
variants with 3-s and 4-s context on each side were worse
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), and 756~s of reference motion did not establish a
long-range motion prior.

Session-level fusion did not reduce this error. One sparse
least-squares problem over all node positions with displacement,
neighbour-velocity and zero-velocity edges leaves the re-read validation error
at 29.7--29.9~mm against 29.67~mm for independent windows, and the ATE within
2~mm, for every weight combination tried; weighting edges by the predicted
variance changes ATE by less than 1.5~mm. The tens of thousands of
displacement edges dominate the few hundred neighbour constraints, and the
velocity stream is constrained only through its integral at the reference
frame times, so velocity edges add little independent information.
Absolute velocity must therefore be supplied by the acquisition, for example
by pauses.

\subsection{Stationary-Boundary Experiment and Acquisition Requirement}
\label{sec:pause}
An epoch correctly identified as stationary supplies the boundary condition
${\bf v}={\bf0}$ and reduces the initial-velocity ambiguity. Detecting it is
reliable: on the validation recordings the stillness
head recovers 94.4--100\% of the frames that the reference confirms slower
than 3~mm/s, with a false-alarm rate of 0--2.3\% on frames faster than
8~mm/s.

Fig.~\ref{fig:zupt}(a) measures what such a boundary condition makes available,
using inertial acceleration with either reference or integrated-gyroscope
attitude and no learned model. Between two detected pauses the velocity is
pinned at both ends; after an attitude trajectory is supplied, the
specific-force samples determine the displacement, and the two boundary
conditions identify one constant board-frame acceleration offset per interval
(solved values 2.9--6.1~mg, dominated by the specific-force magnitude error
along gravity). The only available interval below
2~s gives 0.7~mm with reference attitude and 1.2~mm with gyroscope attitude
($n=1$); this point is illustrative rather than a population estimate. Pooled
2--4-s errors rise to 53.9 and 104.1~mm, and longer intervals produce
hundreds to thousands of millimetres of error. A constant tilt component is
largely absorbed by the fitted constant bias; the remaining divergence
therefore indicates time-varying attitude error, nonconstant bias and sensor
noise. For scale, an uncompensated constant one-degree tilt would leak
0.17~m/s$^2$ of gravity and produce $g_0\,\delta\theta\,t^2/2=0.77$~m of
open-loop position error over 3~s, whereas a tilt growing with a gyroscope
bias $b_g$ gives $g_0 b_g t^3/6$ and is not absorbed by a constant offset.

The benefit that actually materializes is bounded by how often the instrument
stops. The present recordings contain 0.5--2.3 still segments per minute
(training 2.3, validation 1.38, rigidity 1.75, held-out test 0.52), the held-out
test recording contains a single one, and only 16.5\% of validation windows
contain any still frame. Consistently, using the detection as the structural
update (\ref{eq:gate}) improves the mean single-model validation error by
0.26~mm, which is inside the 1.6~mm seed range of the reference
configuration, although it narrows that range to 1.0~mm and improves every
local held-out-test statistic while worsening trajectory error
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Section~\ref{sec:estimators}). Supervising the stillness classifier
without using it in the kinematics does not help, and the two-pass
re-anchoring does not combine with the gate.

A confirmed stationary boundary is thus valuable only over a short span, and
such pauses occur only 0.5--2.3 times per minute in the present recordings. We
therefore hypothesize that raising the rate to 4--6 per minute, a 1--2-s pause
every 10--15~s, would place a pause inside most 7-s windows instead of one
sixth of them and let the stillness gate act more often; the frozen
prospective protocol adopts this rate so that the hypothesis can be tested.

\subsection{Estimators at the Error Floor}
\label{sec:estimators}
Table~\ref{tab:summary} compares every method under one protocol along
twelve evaluation columns, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark} shows the direct-run, held-out-test and per-session
views. On synthetic data, where the sensor model is exact and the
motion and sensor distributions are controlled by the twin, PhysNet
reduces the error of the best public architecture, IMUNet, by 24\%
(16.69 versus 21.89~mm, $R^2$ 0.864 versus 0.812) and by 8\% on the
out-of-distribution split, with one seventh of the parameters (0.42~M versus
2.83~M); all networks run in 0.13--0.16~ms per window, so latency does not
distinguish them.
All architectures trained only on the legacy twin fail on real data; among
those that do not collapse to a zero output PhysNet degrades least (90.2~mm),
and with the gravity-corrected, replay-augmented
twin its synthetic-only model is the only evaluated direct-transfer model to
beat zero motion on real
validation (39.6 versus 42.2~mm, $R^2$ up to 0.17), although fine-tuning from
it brings no further gain.

On real data, where the evaluated architectures approach the empirical floor of
Section~\ref{sec:limit}, the two best do not differ significantly. With the
stillness gate PhysNet has the lowest single-family error on the fixed
validation split (29.67~mm, against 29.91~mm without the gate on the same
training data and 31.90~mm for IMUNet trained on real train only) and in the
leave-one-session-out protocol (fold ensembles 24.95 versus 25.77~mm;
single-model means 25.56 versus 26.38~mm, six of eight sessions won;
Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}(c)). On the held-out test the two are within 0.62~mm
of each other (43.55 versus 42.93~mm); the block-bootstrap 95\% interval of the
PhysNet$-$IMUNet error difference is $[-1.8,\,+3.2]$~mm, and PhysNet has the
lower error in the fastest third (72.7 versus 74.2~mm). Averaging the two ensembles gives
the lowest mean error (equal blend, 42.21~mm, $R^2=0.276$), and the calibrated
blend (weight $w=0.65$, scale $\alpha=1.05$) gives the highest $R^2$ (0.279) and
the lowest fast-third error (72.1~mm) at 42.29~mm. Its improvement over IMUNet,
$[-1.2,\,+2.5]$~mm, contains zero. With about 38 independent 3-s blocks the
recording cannot resolve differences below roughly 3~mm, so it shows neither
superiority nor equivalence. IMUNet, gated PhysNet and the blends beat zero
motion by 6.1--7.5~mm, with improvement intervals that exclude zero
($[2.2,\,10.8]$~mm for gated PhysNet, $[2.6,\,11.4]$~mm for IMUNet and
$[3.3,\,11.9]$~mm for the calibrated blend); Ridge features, the earlier Conv--BiGRU and the fixed-split
RoNIN and TLIO models gain at most 4.9~mm. Under this protocol the proposed
representation is distinguished by efficiency: one seventh of the IMUNet
parameters, the lowest synthetic and OOD errors, and useful
direct transfer, at real-data accuracy that matches the larger models.

The session-level view in Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}(c) is more informative than
either aggregate. On the four large-motion recordings (zero-motion error
37--45~mm) both architectures reach $R^2$ 0.17--0.62, whereas on the three
near-static recordings (16--23~mm) neither improves on zero motion. Data
curation had effects of the same order as architecture: adding the rigidity
recordings as extra training data moved the CV mean of IMUNet from 27.17 to
26.38~mm, and excluding the two rigidity recordings that fail the mount test
moved the single-model validation error of PhysNet from 31.89~mm (two seeds)
to 30.68~mm (five seeds), a change comparable to the seed range.

\subsection{Estimator Performance, Uncertainty, and Physical Consistency}
The heteroscedastic head costs no accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}); it is
evaluated on the two-seed fixed-split ensemble trained with the NLL term, since
the selected CV models use $w_\eta=0$. The raw predicted-plus-ensemble variance
is under-dispersed, covering 32.5\% and 54.5\% of the residuals at one and two
standard deviations, because it is fitted on the dense stream whose
short-horizon errors are much smaller than the 3-s end point. The scale
$\kappa=4.56$ fitted on validation gives 81.4\% and 94.5\% coverage on the same
data (Fig.~\ref{fig:zupt}(b)), against the Gaussian nominal 68.3\% and 95.4\%.
Fitted on one validation recording and scored on the other, it gives 81.0\% and
94.2\%, so the calibration transfers between these recordings. On the held-out
test, whose motion is larger, coverage falls to 63.2\% and 85.2\% (RMS
standardized residual 1.55), so the interval is too narrow outside the
validation distribution. It still discriminates: the most and least confident
deciles have 6.9 and 29.7~mm error on validation and 1.7 and 51.5~mm on the
test.

The sixteen non-gated fold models also estimate the camera--IMU lever arm as
$(-22.6\pm3.8,\,-5.2\pm1.9,\,-29.7\pm7.0)$~mm in camera axes (mean $\pm$
standard deviation across models), a 38-mm offset from the IMU to the left
optical centre, whereas the gated models give $(-20.9,\,-4.1,\,-17.8)$~mm.
Removing the term costs 0.25~mm of validation error at equal weight decay
(32.81 versus 32.56~mm). The estimate is therefore variant-dependent and weakly
constrained by the loss; it is a value to be checked against a mechanical
measurement of the rig, not a calibration.

\paragraph{Trajectory error and the operating point.}
The last column of Table~\ref{tab:summary} and Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectories}
report pose-graph ATE on the held-out test. Chessboard dropouts split the
713 reference nodes into 31 connected components; each is anchored to the
reference position of its first node, so the ATE measures drift over
components of 3.7~s on average rather than over the whole 115-s recording,
and zero motion is scored with the same anchors (119.2~mm). The CV fold
ensemble reduces the ATE of the fixed-split IMUNet from 95.2 to 82.2~mm.
PhysNet gives 87.8~mm with one edge per window but 81.1~mm when every
reference frame in its displacement stream replaces the end-point edges
(29\,885 edges); the non-gated blends give 80.7~mm (calibrated) and 81.1~mm
(equal weights).

The stillness gate separates the two metrics: it improves every local
statistic in Table~\ref{tab:summary} while degrading ATE from 87.8 to 109.8~mm.
Both ensembles are strongly shrunk (median predicted magnitude 15.8 and
16.4~mm against a reference median of 42~mm), so the ATE difference is not a
simple shrinkage effect; the mean local error and the accumulated error weight
the gate's errors differently, and we did not isolate the mechanism. We therefore
report the gated ensemble as the displacement pseudo-measurement and the
non-gated ensemble as the trajectory estimator, rather than selecting one
operating point on a single metric.

\paragraph{Initially anchored relative pose.}
Replacing the reference orientation in the trajectory solve by bias-corrected
gyroscope integration gives a 4.51$^\circ$ orientation RMSE over the 115-s
held-out recording and a 3-s relative-rotation RMSE of 3.48$^\circ$. With the
non-gated calibrated translation blend, the resulting relative-pose stream has
80.59-mm translation ATE and 50.92-mm 3-s translation RPE, against 119.2~mm and
about 60~mm (RMS of the 3-s reference displacement) for zero motion. Attitude
is taken from the reference only at the first node, whereas positions use the
same 31 component anchors as above; the result is therefore a relative pose
stream with periodic position anchoring, not a learned or absolute 6-DoF pose.
