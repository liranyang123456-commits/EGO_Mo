\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The order follows the measurement chain rather than the model: what the
reference certifies, what the twin reproduces, what is observable at this
horizon, what an acquisition property is worth, and only then which estimator
reaches the resulting limit.

\subsection{Measurement-Chain Audits}
Static position repeatability is 0.04--0.06~mm per axis and rotation
repeatability is 0.008--0.035$^\circ$. At 0.25-px corner noise, Monte Carlo
median position spread is 0.80~mm on validation and 0.72~mm on test; rotation
spread is 0.23$^\circ$ and 0.19$^\circ$, respectively
(Fig.~\ref{fig:uncertainty}). Combining static and corner terms by
(\ref{eq:uncertainty}) gives a representative position
$U_{\rm op}(k=2)$ of approximately 1.4--1.6~mm for a frame, excluding the
unavailable board/intrinsic tolerances.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{reference_uncertainty.pdf}
\caption{Reference characterization. Tilt errors produce millimeter-scale
translation changes while remaining weakly visible in reprojection.}
\label{fig:uncertainty}
\end{figure}

Dynamic holdout depends strongly on frame rate: median position residual is
0.27--0.31~mm at 29~fps, 1.06--1.89~mm at 25~fps, and 3.06--5.19~mm at
10~fps. At the latter rate, median rotation residual is 0.96--1.31$^\circ$.
The tilt sensitivity is 3.4--3.7~mm/deg but only 0.13--0.18~px/deg in
reprojection, confirming that a 1-px gate is not a pose-accuracy guarantee.

The rigidity recording yields held-out axis correlations 0.78/0.92/1.00,
magnitude correlation 0.95, and scale 0.99; two earlier recordings made
before the mount was secured fail the test and are excluded. Constraining
PnP rotation to the gyroscope still shifts the test reference by 8.1~mm
median (21.4~mm p90). Training on those contaminated labels raises apparent
0.2-s test $R^2$ from 0.04 to 0.24, directly demonstrating label leakage.

\paragraph{Twin audit.}
All 95 selected inertial sequences and 25 rendered sequences pass the
automated audit. Ideal/noisy recomputation agrees below
$5\times10^{-5}$~g and $10^{-4}$~deg/s per axis. Rendered corner error is
0.252~px median and 0.842~px p90. Fig.~\ref{fig:domain} shows why an initial
twin failed: its median angular rate was three times the real value
(14.1 against 4.3$^\circ$/s) and its translational acceleration was too small.
Retuning these distributions changed single-model real-validation $R^2$ from
0.326 to 0.403.

\subsection{Observable Horizon and the Empirical Error Floor}
\label{sec:limit}
The displacement horizon is the dominant observability parameter
(Fig.~\ref{fig:limit}(a)). At 0.2~s the held-out test does not beat zero motion;
validation $R^2$ rises through 0.177, 0.203, 0.265, and 0.343 for 0.5, 1, 2,
and 3~s, then saturates at 0.339 for 4~s. The selected setting is therefore
$h=3$~s with 2~s context on each side.

At that horizon conventional model changes no longer give a robust gain.
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} lists the fixed-validation choices. Excluding the
deliberately time-reversed data, the Group-A means span 31.89--33.24~mm;
the five-seed reference itself spans 29.80--31.40~mm after the curated
extra-train data are added. Counting the earlier Conv--BiGRU sweeps, more than
forty variants---capacity, regularization, loss, supervision density,
augmentation, pooling, context length, coarse branches, pretraining, input
axes and extra data---show no consistent improvement beyond this seed and
data-selection variation. Time reversal reaches 34.20~mm, confirming that the
real sensor chain is not time-symmetric.

Fig.~\ref{fig:limit}(b) relates the plateau to a low-frequency velocity proxy motivated by
(\ref{eq:decomp}). For every validation pair we compute from the reference the
mean camera velocity $\bar{\bf v}$ over the 7-s window, a robust proxy for the
low-frequency velocity the estimator must guess. The ensemble residual
correlates with $\bar{\bf v}\Delta t$ at $r=-0.55/-0.40/-0.58$ per axis with
slopes $-0.63/-0.49/-0.85$: the network substitutes a zero mean velocity and
inherits the deviation as error, as Section~\ref{sec:observable} predicts.
Knowing $\bar{\bf v}$ would lower the validation error from 31.4 to 26.3~mm,
a 16\% reduction in mean error (about 30\% in squared error). Its magnitude falls with
context length---23.7~mm RMS at 7~s, 11.3~mm at 21~s, 5.1~mm at 61~s---yet the
longer-context variants did not learn to exploit it: 756~s of reference motion
cannot establish a long-range motion prior. The remaining 26~mm are shared
between residual bias and gravity leakage after rotation compensation,
reference noise at 10~fps, and the anisotropic motion prior of the training
recordings.

A session-level estimator cannot repair this after the fact. One sparse
least-squares problem over all node positions with displacement,
neighbour-velocity and zero-velocity edges leaves the re-read validation error
at 29.7--29.9~mm against 29.67~mm for independent windows, and the ATE within
2~mm, for every weight combination tried; weighting edges by the predicted
variance changes ATE by less than 1.5~mm. Two reasons: the tens of thousands
of displacement edges dominate the few hundred neighbour constraints, and the
velocity stream is not identified in absolute terms unless explicitly
supervised, because only its integral relative to the anchor enters the loss.
The missing information has to enter through the measurement.

\subsection{Stationary-Boundary Experiment and Acquisition Requirement}
\label{sec:pause}
An epoch correctly identified as stationary supplies the boundary condition
${\bf v}={\bf0}$ and reduces the initial-velocity ambiguity. Detecting it is
reliable: on the validation recordings the stillness
head recovers 94.4--100\% of the frames that the reference confirms slower
than 3~mm/s, with a false-alarm rate of 0--2.3\% on frames faster than
8~mm/s.

Fig.~\ref{fig:zupt}(a) measures what such a boundary condition makes available,
using inertial acceleration with either reference or integrated-gyroscope
attitude and no learned model. Between two detected pauses the velocity is
pinned at both ends; after an attitude trajectory is supplied, the
specific-force samples determine the displacement and one constant bias per interval becomes
identifiable (solved values 2.9--6.1~mg). The only available interval below
2~s gives 0.7~mm with reference attitude and 1.2~mm with gyroscope attitude
($n=1$); this point is illustrative rather than a population estimate. Pooled
2--4-s errors rise to 53.9 and 104.1~mm, and longer intervals produce
hundreds to thousands of millimetres of error. A constant tilt component is
largely absorbed by the fitted constant bias; the remaining divergence
therefore indicates time-varying attitude error, nonconstant bias and sensor
noise. For scale, an uncompensated one-degree tilt would leak
0.17~m/s$^2$ of gravity and produce 0.77~m of open-loop position error over
3~s.

The benefit that actually materializes is bounded by how often the instrument
stops. The present recordings contain 0.5--2.3 still segments per minute
(training 2.3, validation 1.38, rigidity 1.75, held-out test 0.52), the held-out
test recording contains a single one, and only 16.5\% of validation windows
contain any still frame. Consistently, using the detection as the structural
update (\ref{eq:gate}) improves the mean single-model validation error by
0.26~mm, which is inside the 1.6~mm seed spread of the reference
configuration, although it halves that spread and improves every held-out-test
statistic (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Supervising the stillness classifier
without using it in the kinematics does not help, and the two-pass
re-anchoring does not combine with the gate.

The pause rate therefore becomes a design parameter: a confirmed stationary
boundary can be valuable over a short span, but its measured advantage
disappears as attitude and bias errors accumulate, and such pauses currently
occur only 0.5--2.3 times per minute.
Raising it to 4--6 per minute, a 1--2-s pause every 10--15~s as specified in
the revised protocol, would place a usable epoch inside the context of most
windows instead of one sixth of them and would act on the low-frequency
velocity ambiguity rather than only on the estimator architecture.

\subsection{Estimators at the Limit}
Tables~\ref{tab:multi-syn} and~\ref{tab:multi} compare every architecture
under one protocol along seven evaluation dimensions, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark} shows the direct-run, held-out-test and per-session
views. On synthetic data, where the sensor model is exact and the
motion and sensor distributions are controlled by the twin, PhysNet
reduces the error of the best public architecture, IMUNet, by 24\%
(16.69 versus 21.89~mm, $R^2$ 0.864 versus 0.812) and by 8\% on the
out-of-distribution split, with one seventh of the parameters (0.42~M versus
2.83~M) and lower inference latency than IMUNet (0.21 versus 0.25~ms per
window).
All architectures trained only on the legacy twin fail on real data; PhysNet
degrades least (90.2~mm), and with the gravity-corrected, replay-augmented
twin its synthetic-only model is the only evaluated direct-transfer model to
beat zero motion on real
validation (39.6 versus 42.2~mm, $R^2$ up to 0.17), although fine-tuning from
it brings no further gain.

On real data, where the evaluated architectures approach the empirical floor of
Section~\ref{sec:limit}, the two best are statistically tied. With the
stillness gate PhysNet is the best single family among the evaluated methods on the fixed validation
split (29.67~mm for the five-seed ensemble against 31.90~mm for IMUNet) and in
the leave-one-session-out protocol (25.56 versus 26.38~mm, six of eight
sessions won; Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}(e)), and on the held-out test the two are within
0.6~mm of each other (43.55 versus 42.93~mm) with a block-bootstrap 95\%
interval of $[-3.2,\,+1.8]$~mm for the difference; PhysNet has the lower error
in the fastest third (72.7 versus 74.2~mm) and the higher $R^2$ (0.252 versus
0.250). The two families make partly independent errors. The equal-weight
gate blend gives the lowest local error (42.21~mm, $R^2=0.276$), whereas the
CV-calibrated gate blend (weight 0.65, shrink 1.05) gives the highest
$R^2$ (0.279) and lowest fast-third error (72.1~mm) at 42.29~mm mean error.
The calibrated-blend interval against IMUNet alone,
$[-1.2,\,+2.5]$~mm, contains zero; the held-out recording therefore supports
statistical equivalence, not superiority. Every learned model beats zero
motion by 6--7~mm with intervals that exclude zero
($[2.16,\,10.80]$~mm for gated PhysNet). The
distinguishing property of the proposed representation under this protocol is
its efficiency: one seventh of the IMUNet parameters, lower latency, the
lowest synthetic and OOD errors among the compared methods, useful direct
transfer, and the lowest cross-validated real error.

The session-level view in Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}(e) is more informative than
either aggregate. On the four large-motion recordings (zero-motion error
37--45~mm) both architectures reach $R^2$ 0.17--0.62, whereas on the three
near-static recordings (16--23~mm) neither improves on zero motion. Data
curation matters as much as architecture here: adding the rigidity recordings
as extra training data moved the CV mean of IMUNet from 27.17 to 26.38~mm,
and excluding the two rigidity recordings that fail the mount test moved the
single-model validation error of PhysNet from 31.89 to 30.68~mm---a larger
effect than any architectural variant in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\subsection{Estimator Performance, Uncertainty, and Physical Consistency}
The heteroscedastic head costs no accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Its raw
variance is under-dispersed, covering 32.5\% and 54.5\% of the residuals at
one and two standard deviations, because it is fitted on the dense stream
whose short-horizon errors are much smaller than the 3-s end point. One scale
factor of 4.56 fitted on validation gives 81.4\% coverage within $1\sigma$ and
94.5\% within $2\sigma$ against the Gaussian nominal 68.3\% and 95.4\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:zupt}(b)), with a unit RMS standardized residual. The interval
is therefore conservative at $1\sigma$ and close to nominal at $2\sigma$. It also
discriminates: the most confident decile has a 6.9-mm error against 29.7~mm
for the least confident decile, a factor of 4.3. This is the statement a
pseudo-measurement requires, reported per prediction rather than as an
aggregate.

A second by-product is physically checkable. The sixteen fold models estimate
the camera--IMU lever arm as $(-22.6\pm3.8,\,-5.2\pm1.9,\,-29.7\pm7.0)$~mm in
camera axes (mean $\pm$ standard deviation across models), a 38-mm offset from
the IMU package to the left optical centre; removing the term costs 0.8~mm of
validation error. The value is a testable prediction for the mechanical
drawing and closes the ``provisional lever arm'' entry of the twin.

\paragraph{Trajectory error and the operating point.}
The last column of Table~\ref{tab:multi} and Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectories}
report pose-graph ATE on the held-out test. The CV fold ensembles reduce the ATE
of the fixed-split models by 10--15~mm (IMUNet 95.2 to 82.2~mm). PhysNet gives
87.8~mm with one edge per window but 81.1~mm when every reference frame in its
displacement stream adds an edge (29\,885 edges), and the blends 80.7--81.1~mm.

The stillness-conditioned gate makes the tension between the two metrics explicit: it
improves every local statistic in Table~\ref{tab:multi} while degrading ATE
from 87.8 to 109.8~mm, because suppressing motion increases shrinkage (median
predicted magnitude 15.8~mm against a reference median of 45~mm), which the
mean local error rewards and the accumulated error penalizes. We therefore
report the gated ensemble as the displacement pseudo-measurement and the
non-gated ensemble as the trajectory estimator, rather than selecting one
operating point on a single metric.

\paragraph{Initially anchored relative pose.}
Replacing the reference orientation in the trajectory solve by bias-corrected
gyroscope integration gives a 4.51$^\circ$ orientation RMSE over the 115-s
held-out recording and a 3-s relative-rotation RMSE of 3.48$^\circ$. With the
non-gated calibrated translation blend, the resulting pure-IMU relative-pose
stream has 80.59-mm translation ATE and 50.92-mm 3-s translation RPE. These
values require one initial pose to fix the gauge and do not constitute a
learned or absolute 6-DoF pose result.
