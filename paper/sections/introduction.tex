\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{otion} estimates from cameras with an endoscopic field of
view support navigation, reconstruction, and spatial registration. Their
evaluation, however, presupposes a reference trajectory. Robotic datasets use
forward kinematics~\cite{hayoz2023pose}; other laboratory systems use optical
trackers~\cite{hartwig2022constrained}. Neither is normally
available for a compact handheld camera.

A camera-mounted MEMS IMU is attractive because it is small, inexpensive,
and operates at a higher rate than the camera. Direct integration is
ill-conditioned: gravity, bias, scale factor, axis misalignment, timing, and
initial velocity all enter the position estimate. Data-driven inertial
odometry~\cite{chen2018ionet,yan2020ronin,liu2020tlio,zeinali2024imunet} can
learn motion priors, while online calibration such as
DUET~\cite{liu2023duet} learns sensor corrections. A learned output can be
treated as a measurement only when its reference, calibration and uncertainty
are characterized and when the part of its error that the sensor cannot
observe is quantified.

Three gaps motivate this work. First, learned inertial odometry is normally
reported as an accuracy ranking between architectures. The ranking hides how
much of the residual is unobservable at the chosen window and therefore common
to every architecture, so it cannot tell whether a better network or a better
acquisition is needed. Second, the measurement chain that produces the labels
is rarely characterized: a planar board may reproject accurately while its tilt
and translation remain coupled, and using the same IMU to ``improve'' its
training labels leaks information into the test. Third, a simulated IMU can
cover motion that is tedious to capture, but a digital twin is useful only when
its parameters are fitted to measurements and its domain gap is quantified.
These gaps lead to four research questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[RQ1)] How much of the windowed displacement error is unobservable from
  the IMU at the chosen horizon, rather than unlearned?
  \item[RQ2)] How accurate and repeatable is the planar camera-pose reference
  used for supervision and scoring?
  \item[RQ3)] Which acquisition property could lower the error floor, and what
  does the present data indicate about its size?
  \item[RQ4)] How does a physics-structured representation compare with public
  inertial architectures under one protocol, and does pretraining on the twin
  help on real data?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The contributions are:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item We decompose windowed inertial displacement into an IMU-observable
  double integral and an unobserved initial velocity, and test its
  consequences: more than forty estimator variants share one error plateau,
  predictions are shrunk toward the conditional mean, and neither longer context
  nor session fusion recovers the missing velocity. We also show which
  velocity-proxy tests are and are not conclusive with a 10-fps reference. A
  zero-velocity boundary experiment shows that a confirmed pause helps only over
  intervals of about 2~s under the present attitude solution.
  \item We characterize the camera--IMU measurement chain: the repeatability
  and tilt--translation coupling of the planar reference, a simulated
  known-displacement test of its scale, camera--IMU rigidity on held-out
  increments, and the accuracy inflation caused by IMU-corrected labels.
  \item We propose PhysNet, which combines analytic anchor-frame
  pre-integration with a densely supervised velocity stream, a stillness gate,
  a learned lever arm and a rescaled per-prediction uncertainty. With one
  seventh of the parameters of an adapted IMUNet it has the lowest synthetic
  error and matches IMUNet on real data within the statistical resolution of
  the available recordings.
  \item We build a digital twin with measured sensor parameters and a
  protocol-matched evaluation spanning synthetic test and OOD, direct
  sim-to-real transfer, session-wise cross-validation, a held-out real test
  and trajectory error. Pretraining on the twin, including a realistic
  non-periodic handheld variant, did not lower the real-data error; a protocol
  study in the twin instead identifies the stop rate as the acquisition factor
  that lowers the floor, with sensor noise, look-back and label rate secondary.
\end{itemize}
